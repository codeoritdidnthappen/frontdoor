# EntryMap — the pitch

Four lengths, because the room decides which one you get. Every claim here is inside what the
project can defend; the last section says which sentences are off-limits and why, so a good line
does not get invented under pressure.

---

## Ten seconds

> **Most accessibility apps tell you a place is accessible. EntryMap tells you what a photograph
> shows — and says "I can't see" when it can't.**

---

## Thirty seconds — the elevator

> **Seventy million American adults have a disability. Fewer than six million use a wheelchair —
> so the little wheelchair icon is the wrong question for almost everyone it's meant to serve.**
>
> **Right now the way you find out whether you can get into a shop is to go there.**
>
> **EntryMap is a map of front doors. Take a photo of an entrance and in about seven seconds you
> get what the photograph shows: a ramp, a handrail, a handle you can work with a closed fist,
> accessible signage. It runs in a browser on any phone — no app store, no special hardware.**
>
> **And it's built to say "not visible" instead of guessing, because a wrong answer about
> somebody's front door is worse than no answer.**

---

## Sixty seconds — with the differentiator

Everything above, then:

> **The hard part isn't reading a photograph. It's knowing when not to.**
>
> **Every place on our map carries how we know: estimated from street imagery, scanned on site, or
> confirmed by the business. It never goes backwards, and we never publish a negative — a place is
> either verified accessible or not yet checked. We're not in the business of telling the internet
> that someone's shop is bad.**
>
> **Owners can claim their door and confirm what's there. Anyone can flag something wrong, and it
> reaches a person, not a form.**
>
> **It's live now, with photographs we took ourselves, downtown.**

## The one-liner for a bio or a slide

> **A map of front doors, built from photographs, that admits what it can't see.**

## What makes it different, in one sentence each

Pick whichever the room is asking about.

- **Against a review site:** a review tells you what someone felt about a place; this tells you
  what is at the door.
- **Against an official database:** those are complete where a building was permitted and silent
  everywhere else; a photograph works on any door.
- **Against a wheelchair icon:** one icon collapses seventy million people into one need. Ours
  filters by what *you* need.
- **Against "just use AI to check accessibility":** the difference is the abstention. Anything can
  produce a confident answer; the work is in getting it to stop.

## Sentences that are off-limits, and why

The pitch is only worth anything if it survives the first sceptical question. These are the ways
it stops surviving.

**Never say "97% accurate."** The figure is real but it is **agreement** with a reference a model
produced from the same photographs, so both readings share their blind spots (#437). It is also a
single unrepeated run. Until a human-labelled sample exists, no accuracy number gets said out loud.
"About seven seconds" is safe; it is a latency measurement, not a claim about correctness.

**Never say "compliant", "ADA-compliant", or "meets code."** This screens what a photograph shows.
It is not a measurement and not a legal determination, and one sentence claiming otherwise makes
the whole product a liability.

**Never say a place is inaccessible.** The map has two public states — verified accessible, or not
yet checked. That is a rule, not a limitation, and it is the answer to "aren't you going to get
sued?"

**Never say "step-free entry" is one of the things it checks.** The interface names it; the engine
does not assess it yet (#368). Four criteria: ramp or bevel, handrails, accessible door hardware,
accessibility signage.

**Don't oversell the map's size.** Read the live count off the map on the day rather than quoting a
number from a document.

## The three questions that will come, and the honest answer to each

**"How do you know it's right?"**
> We don't fully, yet, and that's why the app abstains. Our reference for the full set is a second
> reading of the same photographs, which measures consistency rather than truth — so we're
> labelling a sample by hand to get a real accuracy figure with an error bar. What we can show you
> today is that when it can't see something, it says so.

**"What if you're wrong about someone's business?"**
> We never publish a negative. The worst we say is "not yet checked." Anyone can flag a place and
> it reaches a person; an owner can claim their door and confirm what's actually there. And a
> correction can never change a verdict on its own — it can only mark a place as needing another
> look.

**"Why hasn't someone done this?"**
> People have done the database version, and it's complete where buildings were permitted and empty
> everywhere else. The photograph version needed a model that could look at a doorway and be
> trusted to say "I can't tell" — and that's the part that's only recently become possible, and
> still the part that takes the work.
